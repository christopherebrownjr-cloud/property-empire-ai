# Property Empire AI — Master Game Design Document

**Document status:** Foundational blueprint approved; full design-board recommendations accepted for pre-production planning  
**Scope:** Design only; no code, deployment, or production implementation authorized  
**Target difficulty:** Realistic CEO  
**Opening city:** Sacramento, California

## 1. Product vision

Property Empire AI is a narrative business-management simulation about building a property-management and real-estate services company from a one-person operation into a diversified enterprise. The player is a young founder whose decisions shape contracts, communities, employees, capital relationships, reputation, and the company’s long-term path toward a stretch ambition of **$25 billion in annual revenue**.

The game should feel like a credible operating company with a compelling human story—not a spreadsheet disguised as a game. Strategic choices create tradeoffs, relationships unlock opportunities, and every expansion increases organizational complexity.

## 2. Founding decisions — locked

1. **Story origin:** Sacramento, California.
2. **Starting specialization:** The player selects the initial specialization when starting a new save.
3. **World:** Real-world-inspired cities with fictional companies, owners, developers, cities, banks, and competitors.
4. **Major conversations:** Hybrid interaction: authored conversations provide context and personality; strategic options determine commitments, relationships, and consequences.
5. **Presentation priority:** Professional corporate dashboard, supplemented by selective visual scenes of the office, communities, inspections, meetings, and key relationship moments.
6. **Difficulty:** Realistic CEO.

## 3. Realistic CEO difficulty contract

Realistic CEO is the default target experience. It should be demanding because the business is interconnected, not because the game hides information or applies arbitrary penalties.

### Core pressures

- **Cash flow:** Revenue recognition, delayed owner payments, reserves, vendor bills, debt service, taxes, insurance, and working-capital gaps matter. Profitability does not guarantee liquidity.
- **Payroll:** Hiring ahead of revenue creates runway risk; under-hiring creates service failures, overtime, and burnout.
- **Contract competition:** Bids depend on fee structure, experience, references, service capacity, relationships, reputation, and local fit—not simply a hidden score.
- **Financing underwriting:** Banks and lenders assess cash flow, leverage, collateral, guarantees, covenants, concentration risk, and operating history. Strong performance improves terms gradually; weak performance narrows options.
- **Credible occupancy and expenses:** Occupancy changes through pricing, market conditions, property quality, leasing execution, resident experience, seasonality, and competition. Repairs, utilities, turns, vacancies, and capital projects create realistic expense pressure.
- **Employee capacity and burnout:** Each employee has skills, workload, role fit, morale, and community assignments. Overload reduces quality and increases turnover risk; delegation and systems create capacity.
- **Inspections and compliance:** Missed inspections, fair-housing issues, safety deficiencies, documentation failures, and regulatory deadlines create escalating operational and reputational consequences.
- **Reputation:** Owners, residents, cities, lenders, employees, and vendors form partially distinct views of the company. Reputation is earned through repeated behavior and can recover through visible corrective action.

### Failure and recovery

Failure must be legible and recoverable. The player can face lost contracts, refinancing pressure, layoffs, leadership turnover, probationary oversight, or a forced sale of assets. A full game-over state should be reserved for sustained insolvency or an explicitly chosen hard-stop condition. Recovery paths may include restructuring, narrowing the portfolio, bringing in a partner, renegotiating contracts, selling non-core assets, or rebuilding trust over time. Consequences must follow identifiable causes and offer meaningful choices.

## 4. Player fantasy and progression

The player progresses from founder-operator to executive allocator of people, capital, systems, and relationships.

1. **Founder phase:** Personally wins early business, leases units, handles resident issues, builds templates, and learns the local market.
2. **Team phase:** Hires coordinators, leasing staff, maintenance partners, accountants, compliance specialists, and community managers.
3. **Portfolio phase:** Manages multiple communities, service lines, owner expectations, performance reporting, and competing priorities.
4. **Platform phase:** Builds subdivisions such as conventional management, affordable housing, lease-up services, development partnerships, compliance, facilities, technology, and investment/advisory services.
5. **Enterprise phase:** Operates across cities and markets while balancing central standards with local leadership.

The $25 billion revenue ambition is a long-horizon north star, not a guaranteed checklist. Reaching it should require exceptional execution, diversification, financing discipline, and organizational scale.

## 5. New-save specialization choice

The first save begins in Sacramento and asks the player to choose a specialization. Initial options should have distinct economics, rhythms, risks, and story contacts:

- **Market-rate residential management:** Faster leasing feedback, competitive pricing, resident experience, and owner retention.
- **Affordable housing:** Compliance-heavy operations, mission-driven partnerships, funding constraints, inspections, and public stakeholders.
- **Lease-up and development services:** High growth potential, deadline pressure, marketing spend, construction coordination, and contract volatility.
- **Small commercial and mixed-use:** Fewer accounts with larger relationships, tenant improvement exposure, longer sales cycles, and specialized vendors.
- **Facilities and operations services:** Recurring contracts, staffing intensity, service-level agreements, and margin discipline.

Specialization should influence the opening cast, tutorial problems, early contracts, and first strategic opportunities without permanently locking the player out of other divisions.

## 6. Operating model and primary loops

### Daily and weekly loop

Review dashboards → investigate exceptions → prioritize work → make calls or hold meetings → assign employees/vendors → approve spending → monitor outcomes → update owners and staff.

### Growth loop

Build reputation → pursue leads → prepare a bid → negotiate scope and fees → staff the contract → stabilize operations → renew, expand, or lose the relationship.

### Portfolio loop

Track occupancy, collections, work orders, turns, inspections, resident sentiment, owner satisfaction, NOI-related outcomes, staffing, and capital needs. Every metric should explain what changed and what decision can influence it.

### Capital loop

Forecast cash → maintain reserves → approach one of at least five fictional banks/lenders → submit underwriting materials → negotiate terms and covenants → deploy capital → manage repayment and reporting.

## 7. Company, people, and relationships

Employees are named, visible, and assigned to communities and roles. Each profile should show position, manager, capabilities, workload, morale, compensation band, career goals, and current risks. Key characters have relationships with the founder, one another, owners, residents, city officials, vendors, and lenders.

The player should be able to see the organizational structure and community assignments at a glance. Delegation is a strategic system: a strong manager can multiply capacity, but can also become a single point of failure if unsupported.

## 8. Contracts and stakeholders

Contracts define scope, fee model, term, service levels, reporting obligations, incentives, termination rights, capital responsibilities, and renewal conditions. Owners may request amenities, cost reductions, faster lease-up, improved resident satisfaction, or sustainability initiatives within budget.

Stakeholders should have conflicting objectives:

- Owners seek returns, predictability, and asset value.
- Residents seek safety, responsiveness, fairness, and livability.
- Cities seek compliance, stability, and community outcomes.
- Developers seek lease-up velocity and execution certainty.
- Lenders seek repayment, covenants, and credible reporting.
- Employees seek sustainable workloads, growth, and respect.

## 9. Interface and presentation

The corporate dashboard is the primary interface. Recommended modules:

- Executive snapshot: cash, runway, revenue, margin, occupancy, open risks, and top decisions.
- Communities: property performance, alerts, budget, staffing, inspections, and owner sentiment.
- People: employee directory, org chart, capacity, burnout, assignments, and development.
- Contracts: pipeline, bids, terms, renewals, obligations, and relationship health.
- Finance: cash forecast, receivables, payables, debt, covenants, budgets, and scenarios.
- Relationships: owners, banks, cities, developers, vendors, employees, and reputation signals.
- Strategy: new markets, subdivisions, systems, partnerships, and capital allocation.

Selective scenes should punctuate important moments: opening the first office, a difficult inspection, an owner meeting, a community tour, a hiring decision, a bank review, or a crisis response. Scenes should clarify stakes and character, not replace the operating dashboard.

## 10. Narrative structure

The story is organized into business eras and relationship arcs rather than a single linear campaign. The founder’s identity, ethics, ambition, and leadership style evolve through decisions. Major conversations combine authored dialogue with strategic responses such as concede, investigate, delay, escalate, invest, delegate, or walk away.

The game should support multiple viable company identities: disciplined operator, mission-driven housing partner, aggressive growth platform, premium service brand, or diversified institutional manager.

## 11. Economy and simulation principles

The economy should use transparent causal chains and bounded uncertainty. Forecasts should show assumptions and confidence ranges. Random events may create variation, but they should reveal signals, have plausible causes, and allow preparation or mitigation.

Every major metric needs:

1. A player-readable definition.
2. A small number of causal drivers.
3. At least one controllable lever.
4. A lag or tradeoff where appropriate.
5. A path to recovery after deterioration.

Avoid false precision, instant scaling, costless hiring, unlimited debt, magical reputation swings, and “correct answer” dialogue choices.

## 12. Detailed multidisciplinary studio design-board review

### Creative direction

**Strengths:** A distinctive founder-to-enterprise arc, a credible Sacramento launch, and strong room for human stories inside operational decisions.  
**Weak spots:** The $25 billion goal can make early gameplay feel like a tutorial to an abstract number; the premise risks becoming a dry dashboard.  
**Improvements:** Define near-term emotional goals—first retained owner, first promotion, first rescued community, first ethical dilemma—and use scenes sparingly to make operating consequences personal.

### Game design and systems

**Strengths:** Multiple business lines, contracts, people, properties, lenders, and stakeholder conflicts create a rich decision space.  
**Risks:** Too many systems at launch could create cognitive overload and shallow simulation.  
**Improvements:** Build a vertical slice around one specialization, three communities, a small staff, two lenders, and a twelve-month operating arc. Add systems only when they produce a distinct decision.

### Economy and finance

**Strengths:** Cash flow, underwriting, occupancy, payroll, and reserves create meaningful business tension.  
**Weak spots:** Real estate accounting and property-management economics can be confused if corporate revenue, pass-through funds, owner distributions, and property NOI are not separated.  
**Improvements:** Maintain separate ledgers for corporate cash, client/property funds, restricted reserves, receivables, and debt. Make revenue recognition and cash timing visible in plain language.

### Operations and compliance

**Strengths:** Inspections, service levels, fair housing, documentation, and maintenance give the game credible operational texture.  
**Risks:** Compliance can feel punitive or like repetitive paperwork.  
**Improvements:** Use risk-based workflows, checklists, recurring deadlines, audit trails, and early-warning indicators. Let good systems reduce friction rather than merely prevent punishment.

### Narrative and characters

**Strengths:** Employees, owners, lenders, cities, and residents naturally create relationship-driven stories.  
**Weak spots:** Dialogue choices could become cosmetic if they do not change terms, trust, timing, or workload.  
**Improvements:** Tie conversations to explicit commitments and follow-up obligations. Give key characters competing needs and credible boundaries.

### UX and accessibility

**Risks:** A dense dashboard could hide priorities and make the player feel like an analyst rather than a CEO.  
**Improvements:** Use an “explain this” layer, exception-first sorting, saved views, plain-language summaries, color-independent status indicators, keyboard support, and adjustable information density.

### Technical and production planning

**Risks:** A living simulation with many entities can become difficult to balance, test, and save reliably.  
**Improvements:** Define deterministic simulation inputs where possible, version the save schema, log causal events, expose debug summaries for balancing, and establish content/data ownership before implementation.

### Business and ethical design

**Risks:** Housing is a sensitive domain. A game can accidentally reward exploitative practices or imply that residents are merely revenue units.  
**Improvements:** Model resident outcomes, fairness, safety, and compliance as real operating concerns. Offer profitable strategies that also improve livability, and make ethical tradeoffs consequential without preaching.

1. Add cross-city expansion and local-market leadership.
2. Add development partnerships and pre-groundbreaking lease-up work.
3. Add corporate office progression and C-level suite visualization.
4. Add advanced subdivisions, acquisitions, and institutional contracts.
5. Add scenario/modifier modes after the core simulation is balanced.

## 14. Pre-implementation acceptance checklist

- The six founding decisions are treated as locked unless explicitly revisited.
- A new save begins in Sacramento and asks for specialization.
- Fictional firms and banks are clearly distinguished from real-world inspiration.
- Major conversations produce operational or relationship consequences.
- The dashboard is useful without requiring visual scenes.
- Realistic CEO difficulty is demanding, legible, causal, and recoverable.
- The vertical slice can be explained in one page and balanced without the full enterprise.
- No coding or deployment begins until the P0 locks are reviewed and approved.

## 15. Board recommendations — acceptance status

The owner has accepted all recommendations from the multidisciplinary design-board review. They are now part of the approved design direction and should be carried forward into planning, prototyping, balancing, narrative development, UX design, and technical architecture.

Acceptance does not mean every feature must be built at once. The staged priorities remain intentional:

- **P0:** Required foundation before implementation: vertical-slice scope, accounting separation, Realistic CEO rules, core entities/events, and decision-centered dashboard.
- **P1:** High-value systems to follow the foundation: lenders, contract structures, staffing capacity, compliance/reputation escalation, and recovery arcs.
- **P2:** Enterprise depth after the core loop proves fun and legible: cross-city operations, development partnerships, office progression, advanced subdivisions, acquisitions, and scenario modes.

The accepted quality bar is that future systems preserve the board principles: transparent cause and effect, meaningful tradeoffs, credible economics, visible people and relationships, recoverable failure, accessible information, and responsible housing outcomes.
